GP2: Conversational AI for Older Adults

The start of Project 2: a conversational assistant for adults 70 and older. We talked with older adults about technology and memory, narrowed the problem to medication reminders, wrote two personas, and drew the assistant's conversation on paper, including what it does when it doesn't understand and when it hands off to a person.

Needfinding summary

We wanted to learn how older adults feel about the technology they already use, where it lets them down, and whether they would trust an AI assistant to help them. Each of us interviewed an older adult on our own. The two interviews summarized below show two very different levels of comfort with technology.

Participants
Older adults over 70; two summarized here
Method
Interview about everyday technology use, frustrations, and attitudes toward AI
Summary
Ethan Bucy
Consent
Verbal permission to take notes

Older Adult 1: little comfort with phones

  • Wishes technology were more open about changes and updates.
  • Loves the home button.
  • Needs slow, step-by-step, in-person practice before feeling comfortable.
  • Doesn't like it when their information is obviously being sold (personalized ads).
  • Avoids doing anything difficult on the phone because of poor interfaces and compatibility support.
  • Doesn't like autocorrect making mistakes.

Older Adult 2: experienced with technology

  • Uses WordPress to build and run several websites.
  • Reported no problems understanding current technology.
  • Even with a strong grasp of technology, he approves of what AI can do: it has become a respected tool that gives him insurance on his memory when he feels he can't keep track of everything.
  • Wants to understand AI better so it can help him in more parts of his daily life.

Key pain points and accessibility requirements

  • One step at a time. Help has to be slow and step-by-step, not a screen full of options.
  • Predictable and familiar. Updates that move things around cause confusion; a simple, constant "home" to return to is valued.
  • Don't guess for the user. Autocorrect-style mistakes are frustrating, so the assistant should confirm what it heard instead of assuming.
  • Privacy. Personal information should not feel like it is being sold.
  • Memory backup. Even a tech-savvy older adult values AI as a backup for his memory.

Problem statement and scoping

Problem statement. Older adults over 70 can struggle to remember which medication to take, when to take it, and whether they already took it. Phone apps don't solve this for many of them: interfaces change with updates, small steps get confusing, and they want slow, step-by-step help. Our conversational assistant addresses this cognitive challenge with a voice-first medication reminder. It asks which pill and what time, repeats the reminder back to confirm it, checks in at pill time ("Did you take it?"), and alerts a caregiver if a dose is missed.

Why medication reminders

Our workflow sketch has three branches: reminders, a memory game, and phone help. We chose to focus on reminders first because it is the most concrete task, a missed dose has real consequences, and it has a clear point where a person should step in. It also directly answers what our persona Outdated Dan wants: to remember which meds to take and in what order.

In scope

  • Setting a medication reminder by talking: which pill and what time
  • Repeating the reminder back so the user can confirm or correct it
  • A check-in at pill time, and a caregiver alert if the dose isn't taken
  • Fallbacks when the assistant doesn't understand, and calling family after two failed tries

Out of scope for now

  • The family-photo memory game and step-by-step phone help. Both stay on our workflow sketch, and we will add them later if feasible.

User personas

Two fictional personas, one at each end of the comfort range we heard about in our interviews.

Techbite Bill

Bill has lots of experience with technology. He makes his own websites, has AI help him with daily tasks, and FaceTimes his family all the time. Bill has trouble remembering the prompts he puts into AI, and he forgets many of his family members' names. He has tried many AI tools, but he hasn't found the right one for him.

Technical familiarity
High: builds websites, uses AI daily, video calls family
Cognitive considerations
Forgets the prompts he has used and the names of family members
What he needs from the assistant
An assistant that remembers for him, so he doesn't have to remember what to ask

Outdated Dan

Dan doesn't like technology and has no experience with it. He spends most of his time confused when his phone updates or when his children try to FaceTime him. He wants to remember which meds he needs to take and in what order, and he wants help remembering what each of his apps does. He thinks an AI could help, but he doesn't know which one to use.

Technical familiarity
None: confused by phone updates and incoming FaceTime calls
Cognitive considerations
Has trouble remembering which meds to take and in what order, and what each app does
What he needs from the assistant
Simple, spoken help with his medications that doesn't require learning a new app

How the interviews shaped the workflow

  • Older Adult 1 needs slow, step-by-step help, so Phone Help shows only one step at a time, and the fallback repeats more slowly instead of giving up.
  • Autocorrect mistakes frustrate older adults, so the reminder branch repeats back what it heard before saving anything.
  • "Anything else?" always returns the user to the same familiar starting point, like the home button Older Adult 1 loves.
