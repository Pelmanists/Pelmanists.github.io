GP1: Memory Games in VR

A 9-screen VR memory activity for adults 70 and older, built in ForgeXR and played on the Meta Quest 3. Players watch a pattern of glowing colored orbs and repeat it, learn which number goes with each colored shape and match them, then watch the steps of making tea and put them back in order.

Phase 1: Needfinding

We wanted to know where memory actually gets in the way of an ordinary day for an older adult, and what kind of help they would want. Each of us interviewed an older adult on our own, then we met up to compare notes and decide which direction to take the project.

Participants
Three older adults, one per team member
Setting
Separate in-person interviews, followed by a team debrief
Method
Interview about daily routines and memory; notes compared as a team
Interviewers
Caden Dirnberger, Salvador Lomeli Rodriguez, and Ethan Bucy
Consent
Verbal permission to take notes

What we talked about

  • How memory affects their day to day
  • Their daily routines and where those get harder
  • What kinds of things are hardest to remember
  • What kind of help they wish they had

What we heard

  • Memory was harder for them in general, not just now and then.
  • It got in the way of their daily routines, not only of remembering facts.
  • Passwords were hard to remember, and so was how to open certain apps once an update changed the layout.
  • They wished they had some kind of help with it.

How it changed the design

Since memory trouble was an everyday thing, we kept each activity short and repeatable: a screen shows what to remember, and the next one asks for it back (screens 2–3, 5–6, 7–8). Because routines were where it hurt most, the last activity is making tea instead of another abstract pattern. On screens 4 to 6, each color comes with a shape and a number, so players practice remembering one thing by linking it to another. And since they wanted help rather than a test, there are no timers, the animations loop, and Check Answer lets players fix mistakes and try again.

Phase 1: Unplugged prototype

Before opening ForgeXR, we sketched all nine screens on paper. Our first design was a Simon-style game merged with a morning routine: three glowing orbs (red, blue, yellow) light up in a pattern with soft chimes and the player taps them back, then the same idea moves to a kitchen counter, where the kettle, tea bag, and mug light up in order and the player repeats the steps. The sketches mapped each screen's layout, what the player does, and where a success or a mistake leads.

View our paper sketches of all nine screens (PDF)

From paper to ForgeXR

ForgeXR builds lessons from a fixed set of activity types, so parts of the paper design could not be built as drawn. It cannot light up objects on a timer, play chimes, or let the player tap orbs in sequence. We kept the core idea, watch something and then recall it, and adapted it:

  • The timed orb flashes became a looping animation on an Explanation screen, so players can watch as many times as they need.
  • Tapping the orbs back became an Ordering screen where the player arranges the colors.
  • We added a new activity, pairing colors with shapes and numbers on a Matching screen. The shapes also help color-blind players.
  • Tapping kitchen items became dragging the steps of making tea into order.
  • The separate celebration and goodbye screens became one goodbye screen to make room for the new activity.

State map

The session alternates between "watch" screens, where the player takes in something to remember, and "do" screens, where they recall it. Activity screens use ForgeXR's Check Answer, so a player who gets something wrong can rearrange and check again instead of being locked out.

Phase 2: 9-screen system mapping

This table describes the version shown in our demonstration video. Changes we made after testing are listed in Phase 3.

Activity flow, inputs, outputs, and design choices for each screen
# Type Activity Player input System output and next state UX for ages 70+
1Explanation Welcome. A short greeting: today we'll warm up your memory with a few games. Read, then Next Screen. Goes to screen 2. One short message so the player isn't overloaded before starting.
2Explanation (watch) Watch & Learn. An animation of glowing colored orbs lighting up in sequence. Watch the pattern. The animation loops; the player moves on when ready. Looping means no "you missed it" moment; the player can watch as many times as they need.
3Organization: ordering Color Ordering. Five colors: red, yellow, green, purple, blue. Arrange the colors in the order they lit up, then Check Answer. Correct: move on. Mistake: rearrange and check again. Large, labeled blocks; numbers were added to help color-blind players (see Phase 3).
4Explanation Number and color memory explained. Next you'll see blocks with colors, shapes, and numbers; remember them. Read, then Next Screen. Goes to screen 5. Tells the player what to pay attention to before they see it.
5Exploration 3D Blocks. Five blocks, each a color, a shape, and a number. Look around and inspect the blocks. Next Screen goes to matching. Each color also has its own shape, so color vision is not required.
6Matching Number and color matching. Blue circle, green square, red triangle, yellow star, orange octagon to numbers 1 to 5. Match each block to its number, then Check Answer. Correct: move on. Mistake: redo the pairs. Shape names next to colors; five pairs keeps the load manageable.
7Explanation (watch) Recipe sequence for tea. An animation showing the steps of making tea. Watch the steps. Loops; the player moves on when ready. A familiar daily routine, so the memory practice connects to real life.
8Vertical block dragging Recipe sequence: your turn. Four steps: add water to kettle, boil the kettle, pour into mug, pick up mug. Drag the steps into order, then Check Answer. Correct: move on. Mistake: reorder and check again. Short, plain step labels; only four blocks.
9Explanation Goodbye & exit. "You've completed today's session." Read; session ends. End of lesson. A clear, positive ending so the player knows they are done.

Phase 3: Testing and what we changed

We played all nine screens in the Meta Quest 3 and recorded the run with the headset's built-in recorder. Testing in the headset showed several areas that needed troubleshooting and improvement. We fixed them in a revised version of the lesson, listed below.

Problems found after recording, and the fix in the revised version
ScreenProblemFix
3The answer labels included position numbers (for example "Red(1)"), which gave the answer away, and the stored answer key did not match those numbers.Removed the numbers from the labels and corrected the answer key.
5The 3D Blocks screen had no model or hotspots, so the number pairings tested on screen 6 were never actually shown.Added a description and five hotspots ("Blue circle is number 1," and so on); a 3D model still needs to be added.
AllTypos and notes meant for us were visible to players, such as the color-blind note on screen 2.Rewrote every instruction in short, plain language.
9Five of nine screens were reading or watching only.Turned the goodbye screen into a final recall question: "What number was the red triangle?"
9With two questions on one Multiple Choice screen, choosing an answer in one question also selected the same position in the other (a ForgeXR behavior).Kept one question per Multiple Choice screen.
